# M06 — Writing a Marketing Strategy

**Time:** 90 minutes (35 read · 45 exercise · 10 recall)
**Part 1 of 6 · Foundations**

---

## Why this matters

Almost everything called a marketing strategy is a list of tactics with a budget attached. It cannot be argued with, because it makes no claim about why those tactics will work. This module gives you the structure that makes a plan defensible — and the artifact you show an employer or a client to prove you can think, not just execute.

---

## Core concepts

### Rumelt's kernel: what a strategy actually is

Richard Rumelt's *Good Strategy / Bad Strategy* defines a strategy as three parts:

1. **Diagnosis** — what is actually going on. The critical obstacle, named. Not "we need more leads" but "we generate enough leads, and 70% of them are outside our ICP because our only channel rewards volume."
2. **Guiding policy** — the approach chosen to deal with that obstacle, which necessarily rules out other approaches. "Move spend from broad paid social to intent-based search and partnerships in one vertical."
3. **Coherent actions** — a small set of steps that support each other rather than pull in different directions.

Bad strategy has recognisable symptoms: fluff (impressive words with no content), failure to face the problem, mistaking goals for strategy ("grow revenue 40%" is a goal, not a plan), and a scatter of unrelated objectives that quietly compete for the same people and money.

The test: **does your strategy say what you will not do?** If not, it is a wish list.

### Objectives, goals, KPIs

Sloppy vocabulary here causes real confusion in meetings.

| Term | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Objective | The qualitative outcome you want | Become the default choice for independent physio clinics |
| Goal | The measurable version, with a number and a date | 120 new clinic customers by 31 December |
| KPI | The ongoing metric you steer by | CAC payback, qualified leads/week |
| Metric | Anything you can count | Impressions, sessions, followers |

Most KPIs are metrics with ambition. A real KPI changes a decision when it moves; impressions almost never do. Pick three at most, including one **leading** indicator you can influence this month (qualified leads, trial starts) and one **lagging** one that proves it worked (revenue, retention).

### Channel focus beats channel spread

The commonest failure in small-budget marketing is doing eight channels badly. Every channel carries a fixed learning cost: time to understand it, creative to build, a minimum spend before the data means anything. Split a small budget eight ways and you pay eight learning costs and clear no thresholds.

Pick **one or two channels** and go deep until they genuinely work, then add a third. Choose on where your buyers already are (M02), whether the channel captures or creates demand (M01), and whether your unit economics can afford it (M05).

The exception is dependence risk: a business drawing 90% of revenue from one channel is one algorithm change from a crisis. Once a channel works, build a second deliberately — not by scattering.

### Budget heuristics: 70/20/10 and 60/40

**70/20/10**: 70% to what is proven, 20% to promising things being scaled, 10% to genuine experiments. It blocks two failure modes at once — over-investing in unproven ideas, and never testing anything until the proven channel decays. On a small budget the 10% may be time rather than money.

**60/40** comes from Les Binet and Peter Field's work on the IPA effectiveness databank: roughly 60% of budget to long-term brand building and 40% to short-term activation maximises effectiveness over time. Brand builds slowly and lifts everything; activation converts demand that already exists without creating more.

Caveats, because this gets quoted as gospel:

- It is an *average across many campaigns*. B2B and considered purchases sit nearer 50/50 in Binet and Field's later work.
- It assumes enough budget for brand work to reach anyone. Below a certain scale, splitting achieves neither.
- Early-stage businesses need a working acquisition loop first; brand-heavy spend before product-market fit buys awareness for something people do not yet want.

Use it as a corrective against the pull toward 100% activation, which is what happens when only measurable things get funded.

### Seasonality and planning cycles

Map demand across the year before allocating. Most categories have a shape: tax season, back to school, January fitness, Q4 retail, B2B budgets closing at year-end. Two rules follow. **Advertise into the season, not during it** — awareness needs lead time, and launching in the peak week means highest costs and finished consideration. And **never diagnose a channel on a seasonal trough**; compare year on year.

Plan annually for objectives and budget, quarterly for campaigns and experiments, and leave 10–20% unallocated for opportunities and mistakes.

### How to say no

Most requests arrive with sincerity and no numbers. Three useful responses:

- **"What would we stop to do this?"** Makes the trade-off visible instead of extending an infinite list.
- **"Which objective does this serve, and what would we see if it worked?"** Filters out activity that exists to look busy.
- **"Can we test it inside the 10%?"** Converts an argument into an experiment with a budget cap.

Write down what you declined and why. That list is your strategy's evidence, and it stops you re-litigating the same argument quarterly.

### Presenting it to a boss or client

Lead with the diagnosis, not the tactics:

1. What is happening and why (diagnosis, evidence from M02 and M05)
2. The one thing we are betting on (guiding policy)
3. What we will do — and what we are deliberately not doing
4. What it costs and what we expect back, arithmetic shown
5. How we will know in 90 days, and what would make us stop

Point 5 buys more credibility than any projection: naming a kill condition in advance signals you are not asking for open-ended faith.

## How it works in practice

A real strategy document is one to three pages. Longer does not get read, and length correlates with vagueness rather than rigour. The annual version takes a week of thinking and an afternoon of writing; the quarterly refresh takes an hour. The time goes into the arithmetic and the arguments about what to drop.

---

## 2026 reality check

- **Tactics expire faster than ever; the kernel does not.** Channel-specific plans go stale within a year — an argument for a diagnosis-led plan whose actions can be swapped without rewriting the logic.
- **AI removes the excuse for a thin diagnosis.** Synthesising reviews, transcripts and competitor messaging is cheap now, so a plan opening with generic market claims looks lazier than it did three years ago.
- **Attribution weakness changes planning.** With channel-level measurement degraded (M13, M15), good plans commit to a budget split and a holdout test rather than promising per-channel ROI.

---

## Common mistakes

- **A tactics list with a budget** and no diagnosis.
- **Goals presented as strategy.** "Grow 40%" tells nobody what to do on Monday.
- **Too many objectives.** Three or more priorities means none.
- **Eight channels, no depth.**
- **All activation, no brand** — profitable for two quarters, then acquisition costs climb and nobody knows why.
- **No kill criteria**, so failing initiatives run until someone loses patience.

## Exercise (45 minutes)

Write the one-page strategy for **your chosen business** using `templates/marketing-plan-1pager.md`, pulling from M02–M05.

1. **Diagnosis** (10 min). One paragraph naming the single critical obstacle, with evidence. Ban "increase," "improve" and "grow" here — they describe outcomes, not problems.
2. **Guiding policy** (5 min). One or two sentences, stated so that it excludes something.
3. **Coherent actions** (10 min). Three to five that support each other. Owner and date on each.
4. **Objectives, goals, KPIs** (5 min). One objective, two goals with numbers and dates, three KPIs including one leading and one lagging.
5. **Budget** (5 min). Split by channel and by 70/20/10. Check every channel against your max affordable CPA from M05.
6. **Not doing** (5 min). Five things you are declining, one line of reasoning each.
7. **Kill criteria** (5 min). Per action: what should be visible by day 90, and what result would stop it?

## If you remember one thing

**A strategy is a diagnosis, a guiding policy, and coherent actions — and it must say what you will not do.** A list of tactics with a budget is not a strategy, however confident the deck looks.
