# M04 — Product, Price, Place: The Offer

**Time:** 60 minutes (25 read · 25 exercise · 10 recall)
**Part 1 of 6 · Foundations**

---

## Why this matters

Price is the only lever that drops straight to profit — no production cost, no media cost. Most businesses set it by copying a competitor or adding a markup to cost, the two worst methods available. Meanwhile the offer wrapped around the product, and the places it can be bought, move sales more than any campaign.

---

## Core concepts

### A product is not an offer

The product is what you make. The **offer** is everything weighed at the moment of decision: the product, **price and payment terms**, **risk reversal** (guarantee, free returns, pilot, easy cancellation), the **bundle**, **onboarding and support**, real urgency, and **proof**.

Two businesses can sell an identical product at very different conversion rates because one has an offer and the other has a price list. If you cannot change the product you can nearly always change the offer — faster and cheaper than a new campaign. Most offer components are anxiety-reducers (M02), not new benefits.

### Three ways to price, one of which is good

| Method | Problem |
|---|---|
| **Cost-plus** (cost × markup) | Your costs are irrelevant to the buyer. Penalises efficiency, caps you at your own cost structure. |
| **Competitor-based** (match or undercut) | Outsources strategy to a rival whose costs and segment you do not know. Races to the bottom. |
| **Value-based** (price against the buyer's alternative) | Needs research. Almost always a higher, more defensible number. |

Value-based pricing asks: *what does the buyer's next-best option cost in money, time and risk, and what is our improvement worth?* If your tool saves a bookkeeper six hours a month, the reference is six hours of a bookkeeper — not your server bill. Cost sets the floor and your contribution margin (M05); it should not set the price.

### Price is a positioning signal

Buyers read price as quality information, especially when they cannot evaluate beforehand — services, health, B2B software, anything bought rarely. A price far below the category norm reads as "something is wrong," not "bargain."

So **cheap is a position, and a hard one.** It requires a real structural cost advantage — scale, automation, a cheaper channel. Without one, low price is a smaller margin funding the same work, and it attracts the least loyal, highest-support customers.

### Elasticity, intuitively

You rarely need to compute elasticity, only to know which side you are on. **Inelastic:** urgent problems, small share of budget, few substitutes, high switching cost, someone else paying. **Elastic:** easy comparison, many substitutes, discretionary, price-visible marketplaces. Small businesses under-price because they imagine an elastic market when their buyers are actually choosing on trust and availability. If nobody ever objects to your price, it is too low.

### The arithmetic of discounting

A discount comes entirely out of margin. Sell at $100 with a $60 cost — gross margin $40.

- **20% off** → price $80, margin $20. You need **twice the volume** for the same gross profit.
- **10% off** → price $90, margin $30. You need **33% more volume**.

Run this on your own numbers once and you will discount less. Discounts also train buyers to wait, and habitual sales permanently reset the reference price. If you must move volume, prefer bonuses and bundles — they cost you at *your* cost, not at retail, and leave the price intact.

### Freemium, trials, tiers

**Free trial:** best when value is obvious fast and the product is self-serve; time-limited usually beats feature-limited. **Freemium:** a permanent free tier is a distribution strategy, not a pricing one — it works only when free users generate referrals, network effects or data *and* serving them is nearly free. Otherwise you have bought a support obligation. **Tiers:** three is the usual sweet spot; tier on a value metric that grows with the customer (seats, volume, revenue) so revenue rises without a renegotiation.

### Anchoring and decoys, done honestly

The first number seen frames every judgement after it, so showing a premium tier first makes the middle tier feel reasonable. A decoy is a deliberately weaker option that flatters the target one. Both are real effects, and legitimate **only if every option is genuinely purchasable and honestly described**. Fake "was" prices and permanent countdown timers are illegal in many jurisdictions and burn the trust you need for the second sale. The honest version: order options so the best-value one is easy to recognise.

### Place: distribution

| Channel | Strength | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Owned site or shop | Full margin, own the customer data | You generate all demand yourself |
| Marketplaces | Existing demand, built-in trust | Fees, no relationship, platform risk |
| Partners and affiliates | Borrowed audience and credibility | Margin share, less message control |
| Retail / wholesale | Reach and physical availability | Deep discounts, slow payment, listing fees |

Being easy to buy beats being loudly advertised. A shelf at eye level, a listing where the category is searched, an integration inside software the buyer already uses — these convert demand promotion would otherwise have to create. That is Sharp's physical availability, and it is why fixing distribution before raising ad spend usually wins.

The trade-off is control: someone else's channel means their rules and fees, and their right to change both. Marketplace-only businesses rent a customer base — build an owned channel in parallel from day one.

## How it works in practice

Price gets revisited far too rarely, usually only in a panic. Healthier: review annually and after any material change in cost, competitor set or value delivered. Test increases on new customers first, grandfather existing ones for a period, and announce with a reason and notice. Price rises lose fewer customers than founders fear, and the ones lost are the least profitable.

## 2026 reality check

- **Comparison is instant and conversational.** AI assistants line up prices and alternatives side by side, raising elasticity in commodity categories and rewarding clear published pricing. Hiding price behind "contact us" increasingly means being left out of the comparison.
- **Rising ad costs make margin decisive.** Only businesses whose contribution margin can fund a CAC (M05) survive paid acquisition. A price increase is often the fastest way to make a channel affordable.
- **Platform dependence is a live risk.** Fee changes and algorithm shifts have killed otherwise healthy businesses. An owned email list is cheap insurance.

---

## Common mistakes

- **Pricing from cost.** Your costs are your business, not the buyer's concern.
- **Competing on price with no cost advantage.** A slow retreat, not a strategy.
- **Discounting instead of improving the offer.** Guarantees and bundles cost less and do more.
- **Never raising prices** while costs rise, then needing a painful 30% correction.
- **One price for everyone** when two segments value you very differently.
- **Treating distribution as logistics.** It is a marketing decision with the largest multiplier.

## Exercise (25 minutes)

For **your chosen business**:

1. **Write the current offer in full** (5 min) — product, price, terms, guarantee, bundle, onboarding, proof. Note what is missing.
2. **Rewrite it** (10 min), strengthening at least two components — ideally a risk reversal aimed at the biggest anxiety from M02.
3. **Set a price with written rationale** (5 min): buyer's next-best alternative and its cost, value delivered, your cost floor, the position the price signals. One paragraph.
4. **Run the discount math** (2 min) on your real margin: what volume does a 20% discount require?
5. **List every place you can be bought** (3 min), plus one you are not in. What would entry cost in margin?

**Fix the offer before you fix the ad, and price against the buyer's alternative rather than your own costs.** Then make sure it is genuinely easy to buy.
