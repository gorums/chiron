# M03 — Positioning, Brand, Messaging

**Time:** 90 minutes (35 read · 45 exercise · 10 recall)
**Part 1 of 6 · Foundations**

---

## Why this matters

Positioning is the decision that makes every later decision easier or impossible. Weak positioning shows up as ads that need three sentences to explain what you are, a sales team inventing its own pitch, and a price you cannot defend. You cannot fix it downstream with better copy.

---

## Core concepts

### Positioning is context, not a slogan

Positioning is the context you place your product in so its value is obvious to the right person. A hammer is unremarkable in a hardware store and remarkable on a desert island.

### April Dunford's five components

The most usable framework in the discipline. Work it in this order — the order *is* the method:

1. **Competitive alternatives.** What would customers do if you did not exist? Include spreadsheets, an intern, an agency, and doing nothing — taken from M02 interviews, not a competitor grid.
2. **Unique attributes.** What do you have that the alternatives do not? Facts only, no adjectives.
3. **Value.** So what? Translate each attribute into an outcome. If an attribute maps to no value, drop it.
4. **Who cares most.** Which customers care intensely about that value? Their shared characteristics are your best-fit segment, usually narrower than you want.
5. **Market category.** What context makes your value obvious fast?

Most positioning work fails because people start at step 5 — picking a category they like — and reverse-engineer the rest.

### Category entry vs. category design

**Entry**: compete inside a category buyers already understand ("project management tool"). Cheap, because the buyer needs no education, but you must be visibly different on something they already care about.

**Design**: name and teach a new category. Enormous upside, enormous cost — you fund the education of the market and competitors then enter for free. Sensible only when the existing category misprices you or attracts the wrong buyer.

Default to entry with a sharp sub-segment. Beginners reach for category design because it sounds ambitious; usually it avoids the harder work of being genuinely different inside a category people already search for.

### The positioning statement template

For **[best-fit segment]** who **[trigger]**, **[product]** is the **[category]** that **[key value]**, unlike **[primary alternative]**, because **[unique attribute]**.

An internal alignment tool, not a headline. If it does not fit on one line, the positioning is not decided yet.

### Differentiation vs. distinctiveness

**Differentiation** is being meaningfully different on something buyers value. It affects choice.

**Distinctiveness** — Byron Sharp's contribution — is being *recognisable*: colours, logo, characters, sonic cues, a format, a phrase. It affects whether you are noticed and correctly attributed at all.

Sharp's work shows that in most categories buyers do not perceive brands as very different, and buy largely out of habit and availability. Growth comes from **mental availability** (coming to mind in a buying situation) and **physical availability** (being easy to buy). Distinctive assets build the first.

The uncomfortable implication: **being noticed and remembered usually beats being cleverly different.** Consistency beats novelty, and most brands change their campaign long before customers have registered it.

Do not over-apply this. Sharp's evidence is strongest in large, low-involvement, repeat-purchase consumer categories. In B2B, small niches, and expensive considered purchases, real differentiation and a specific ICP matter more. Use both: differentiate on substance, then repeat your distinctive assets relentlessly.

### Message hierarchy

- **One core message** — the single thing you want remembered. If you have three, you have none.
- **Three supporting pillars** — the reasons the core message is true.
- **Proof under each pillar** — numbers, named customers, demos, certifications, guarantees, screenshots.

Proof is the layer almost always missing. A claim with no proof reads as noise; a claim with a number and a named customer reads as fact. Every asset then draws from the hierarchy — an ad uses the core message and one pillar, a landing page all three — which is how consistency happens without everyone writing the same page.

### Features, benefits, outcomes

- **Feature:** automatic bank-feed reconciliation.
- **Benefit:** your books stay accurate without manual entry.
- **Outcome:** you close the month in a day instead of a week.

Outcomes sell; features prove. Beginners list features. Over-corrected marketers write outcome fluff with nothing concrete and become indistinguishable from every competitor. Pair them: *outcome, then the feature that makes it believable.*

### "We're the best" is not positioning

Superiority claims without a comparison set, a segment, or proof are noise: best-in-class, world-class, innovative, seamless, next-generation. Test any line by asking whether a competitor could put it on their site. If yes, delete it. Positioning must be falsifiable — it must exclude someone.

### Naming, taglines, and brand as memory

A name must be pronounceable, spellable after one hearing, and searchable. Descriptive names help early discovery; abstract names give room later. Neither is a moat. A tagline either states the position or carries the feeling; what it must not be is a riddle. If a customer cannot say what you do after reading your homepage headline, it has failed however nice it sounds.

A brand is not a logo or a personality document. It is the set of associations that fire when a buying situation arises — built by repeating consistent, distinctive cues, decaying when you stop. A strong one lowers acquisition cost, raises acceptable price and shortens sales cycles, which is why cutting brand spend looks free for two quarters and expensive in year two.

## How it works in practice

Positioning is a two-week job, not a two-hour one: interviews (M02), a session running Dunford's five steps, a draft, then testing it on five people who do not work for you — ideally recent buyers and recent losses. The test is comprehension, not applause: ask them to explain back what you do and who it is for. Then roll it into website, deck, ads and onboarding in the same month, or you will run three positions at once.

---

## 2026 reality check

Discovery increasingly runs through AI answer engines that summarise a category and name a handful of options. Those summaries are assembled from how you *and others* describe you: your site, review sites, forums, comparison articles, press.

- **Ambiguous positioning is now a retrieval failure, not only a comprehension failure.** If your site never plainly states "X is a [category] for [segment]," a model has nothing unambiguous to lift.
- **Repetition across sources beats clever phrasing on your homepage.** The brand that gets named is the one described the same way in many places, so third-party corroboration — reviews, directories, comparison pages — is leverage.
- **Category choice is higher-stakes.** A category nobody asks about means you never appear in the answer.

Say the same unambiguous thing everywhere, for longer than feels necessary. Which is also Sharp's advice for humans.

## Common mistakes

- **Positioning against a competitor instead of the real alternative**, which is usually the status quo.
- **Choosing the category you want** rather than the one buyers search in.
- **Changing the message because you are bored of it.** Internal fatigue arrives long before customer awareness.
- **Claiming three things at once.** Nothing lands.
- **Feature lists with no outcome, or outcome fluff with no proof.**
- **Positioning by committee** — a statement true of everyone and exciting to nobody.

## Exercise (45 minutes)

For **your chosen business**, using the M02 quotes:

1. **Run Dunford's five steps in order** (20 min): alternatives (include "do nothing") → unique attributes (facts only) → value → who cares most → category. Write the honest version, not the flattering one.
2. **Fill the positioning statement template** (5 min). One line. Rewrite until it fits.
3. **Build the message hierarchy** (15 min): one core message, three pillars, concrete proof under each. If a pillar has no proof, find proof or cut the pillar.
4. **Delete test** (5 min): take your homepage (or a competitor's) and strike every line a competitor could also claim. What survives is your real differentiation. If nothing survives, that is the finding.
5. Name **three distinctive assets** you will repeat for at least a year — colour, shape, phrase, format, character, sound.

## If you remember one thing

**Positioning is a decision about context and exclusion, not a slogan.** Decide who you are not for, state it the same way everywhere, and repeat it long past the point where you are sick of it.
