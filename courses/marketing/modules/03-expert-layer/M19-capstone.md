# M19 — Capstone: A Complete Go-to-Market Plan

**Time:** 120 minutes (15 setup · 90 writing · 15 self-assessment)
**Part 6 of 6 · Expert Layer**

---

## Why this matters

Nobody can see what you know. They can only see what you produce.

Eighteen modules of understanding are worth very little in a job interview, a client conversation, or a bank meeting until they are compressed into a document that shows judgment. This module produces that document — a go-to-market plan for one real business, written to the standard a hiring manager, a client, or a co-founder would take seriously.

Writing also forces integration. You will discover in the next two hours that your positioning does not survive contact with your channel choice, or that your unit economics rule out the channel you assumed you would use. That collision is the point, and it is exactly the thinking that separates someone who runs campaigns from someone who decides what the campaigns should be.

Use the same business you have used throughout. If you have been switching, pick one now and commit.

---

## What you are producing

**A 6–10 page go-to-market plan** for one business, written for a reader who is smart, busy, and not a marketer — a CEO, an investor, a client, or an interviewer.

Standards:

- **Decisions, not options.** "We will do X because Y" beats "we could consider X, Y or Z." A plan that refuses to choose is not a plan.
- **Numbers everywhere they belong.** Prices, conversion rates, CAC, CPA ceilings, budget lines, targets. Estimates are fine; unlabelled estimates are not. Mark each assumption as measured, benchmarked, or guessed.
- **Explicit exclusions.** What you are not doing, and why, is the strongest signal of judgment in the document.
- **Uncertainty stated, not hidden.** Name what you do not know and what would change your mind. Confident vagueness is what experienced readers screen for.
- **Plain language.** No jargon that does not earn its place.

Length is a constraint, not a target. If it runs to 15 pages, cut — the discipline of fitting it into 10 is part of the exercise.

## The deliverable, section by section

### 1. Situation and diagnosis (~1 page)

What the business is, what it sells, to whom, at what price, and how it makes money today. Then the diagnosis: **the single most important constraint on growth**, stated as a constraint rather than a wish (M01, M06).

"We need more sales" is not a diagnosis. "We convert 4% of demo requests to paid, against a 12% category norm, and we have no idea why" is. "Nobody in our category knows we exist and 90% of our leads come from one referral partner" is. Everything downstream should follow from this sentence — if a later section does not address the constraint, ask why it is in the plan.

Include a short competitive picture: the three real alternatives your buyer considers, including doing nothing and doing it manually.

### 2. ICP and jobs-to-be-done (~1 page)

Who exactly you are for, and who you are explicitly not for (M02). Firmographics or demographics, but more importantly the **situation** they are in, the **trigger** that starts them looking, what they have already tried, and the job they are hiring your product to do.

Include at least three verbatim or realistically-reconstructed customer phrases. If you have run interviews, quote them; if not, say so and mark it as an assumption to validate. That honesty scores higher than invented certainty.

For B2B, add the buying committee: who else is in the room and what each needs (M16).

### 3. Positioning and message hierarchy (~1 page)

A positioning statement in a fixed structure (M03):

> For [ICP] who [situation/trigger], [product] is the [category] that [primary differentiated benefit], unlike [primary competitive alternative], because [proof].

Then the message hierarchy: one primary message, three supporting messages, and the proof point under each. Add a short "what we do not say" list — claims you are deliberately leaving to competitors.

Test it before moving on: could a competitor put their name in your positioning statement and have it still read true? If yes, it says nothing.

### 4. Offer and pricing (~0.5–1 page)

What is sold and at what price, with a rationale (M04): price points, packaging or tiers, what is included, the entry offer, and the guarantee or risk-reversal. State the pricing logic — cost-plus, competitive, or value-based — and include gross margin, because every economic decision later depends on it.

### 5. Funnel model, unit economics, and max CPA (~1 page)

A funnel with real or estimated rates at each step, from first touch to paying customer (M05). Then:

- **AOV or ACV**, and gross margin
- **LTV** on gross profit, with the retention or repeat-rate assumption behind it, capped at a defensible horizon (M17)
- **Max CAC** at your chosen LTV:CAC ratio, and the reasoning for that ratio
- **Payback period**, and whether your cash position tolerates it
- **Max CPA per funnel step**, worked backwards — the number you will actually bid against

Worked, with the arithmetic visible. This is the section that most separates strong plans from weak ones, and the one interviewers probe hardest.

### 6. Channel strategy (~1–1.5 pages)

**Two or three channels. Not five.** For each: why this channel for this ICP, what role it plays (creation or capture, M01), what the first campaign looks like concretely, the expected cost per outcome against your max CPA, and how long before you would know if it works.

Then the section that demonstrates seniority: **exclusions.** Name at least three channels you are not using, with the specific reason — the audience is not there, the unit economics do not support the CPC, you lack the volume to make it work, the cycle is too long, you cannot produce the creative it demands. Vague dismissal does not count.

Reference 2026 conditions where they bear on the choice: black-box buying means creative and offer are your levers (M11, M15); ~60% of searches ending click-free means organic strategy must include being named in answers, not only ranking (M08, M18).

### 7. The 90-day plan (~1–1.5 pages)

Weeks 1–12 in a table, with a named owner and a deliverable per week, grouped into phases — typically foundations and tracking, then launch, then optimise.

Be concrete. "Improve SEO" is not a week's work; "publish three comparison pages targeting [terms] and add schema markup" is. Include the unglamorous foundations — analytics and key events, UTM convention, CRM stages, consent banner — and what has to be true before each phase can start.

### 8. Budget allocation (~0.5 page)

A table: channel, monthly spend, purpose (creation or capture), expected outcome, confidence, evidence type (M15). Built objective-and-task, from the target backwards, not as a percentage. Include a line for creative production and one for tools. State what you would cut first at −20% and where the money would go at +50%; both answers reveal how well you understand marginal returns.

### 9. Measurement plan (~1 page)

- **The one metric that matters,** decomposed two levels into a metric tree with leading and lagging nodes marked (M13)
- **Key events** with values, and which single one is the optimisation target you feed the ad platforms
- **UTM convention** — a closed list of `medium` values, campaign naming pattern with dates, casing rule, owner
- **Reporting cadence** — daily, weekly, monthly, quarterly
- **Attribution stance** — three or four sentences on what you will and will not trust, and the one incrementality test you run first (branded search or retargeting is usually the right answer)
- **Self-reported attribution** — the exact "how did you hear about us" question you will add

### 10. Experiment backlog (~0.5 page)

Five to eight hypotheses in the because / we expect / measured by format (M14), ICE-scored and ranked.

For the top one, add the sample size reality check: baseline rate, MDE, traffic required, weeks needed. If you cannot test it, say so and state the alternative method. **A plan that admits it lacks the traffic to A/B test scores higher than one that pretends otherwise.**

### 11. Risks and what would change course (~0.5 page)

Four to six risks, each with a mitigation and — critically — a **trip-wire**: the observable condition that would make you change the plan. "If CPA on Meta prospecting exceeds £X for three consecutive weeks with acceptable creative volume, we stop and shift budget to Y." "If month-3 cohort retention stays below 30%, we pause acquisition scaling and work on onboarding." Include at least one external risk (platform change, competitor, regulation) and one internal one (capacity, cash, key-person dependency).

## Self-assessment rubric

Score each row 1–3. Be harsh; the score is for you.

| # | Section | 1 — Weak | 2 — Competent | 3 — Strong |
|---|---------|----------|---------------|-----------|
| 1 | Situation & diagnosis | Describes the business; no constraint named | Constraint named but generic | One specific constraint, evidenced, and every later section traces back to it |
| 2 | ICP & JTBD | Broad demographics; "small businesses" | Clear segment with a trigger | Segment, situation, trigger, exclusions, and real customer language |
| 3 | Positioning | Generic benefit claim | Fills the template correctly | A competitor could not substitute their name; proof is specific and real |
| 4 | Offer & pricing | Price stated with no rationale | Price with logic and margin | Packaging, entry offer, risk-reversal, and margin that supports the plan |
| 5 | Unit economics | Missing or hand-waved | CAC and LTV computed | Full chain to max CPA per step, on gross profit, assumptions labelled, payback addressed |
| 6 | Channel strategy | Lists every channel | 2–3 chosen with reasons | Chosen with reasons, roles assigned, CPA-tested, and three exclusions justified specifically |
| 7 | 90-day plan | Vague themes | Weekly actions | Weekly actions with owners, dependencies, phases, and foundations included |
| 8 | Budget | A percentage or a round number | Allocated by channel | Objective-and-task, with confidence per line and answers for −20% and +50% |
| 9 | Measurement | Lists tools | Metric tree and key events | Metric tree, UTM convention, cadence, honest attribution stance, first incrementality test named |
| 10 | Experiments | Ideas listed | Hypotheses in correct format, prioritised | Sample size reality check done, and an honest alternative where testing is not possible |
| 11 | Risks | Generic risks | Risks with mitigations | Risks with observable trip-wires and a stated course change |
| 12 | Coherence | Sections contradict each other | Internally consistent | Reads as one argument; the diagnosis drives every choice |

**Under 24:** the plan is a description, not a strategy. Rework sections 1, 3 and 5 first.
**24–30:** solid working plan. Pick your three lowest rows and improve them.
**31–36:** portfolio-grade. Have someone who knows the business challenge it, and make sure you can defend every number out loud.

Then write your **improvement list**: the three lowest-scoring rows, one concrete action each, and a date.

## How to use this artifact

**As a portfolio piece.** Strip anything confidential, put it in a clean PDF, and make it the thing you send when someone asks what you can do. One well-reasoned plan beats a list of courses you have taken.

**In job interviews.** You will be asked "how would you approach marketing for us?" Do not answer generically — answer with your method, demonstrated: "Here is a plan I built end to end; let me walk you through how I diagnosed the constraint and why I excluded three channels." Then be ready for the pressure test — where the numbers came from, what you would do at half the budget, how you would know in 90 days if you were wrong. Sections 5, 8 and 9 are where interviews are won.

**In client proposals.** Sections 1, 2, 3 and 7 are the shape of a paid discovery deliverable. The fastest route to freelance credibility is to do this unpaid for one prospect's business, present it, and let the quality of the diagnosis sell the engagement.

**Running your own business.** Print sections 7, 8 and 9 and work from them, then revisit every quarter: update the funnel rates with real data, retire assumptions that turned out wrong, rescore the rubric. A plan that is never revised was never a plan.

## What comes after hour 30

You are now, genuinely, more useful than most people who list marketing on their CV — because you can do the arithmetic, you know what attribution cannot tell you, and you know what you do not know.

What you do not have is reps. Judgment comes from having been wrong in specific, memorable ways, and thirty hours cannot manufacture that.

The next step is one real thing, run for one quarter, measured honestly: a small budget you actually spend, a landing page you actually ship, an email flow that actually sends, a holdout you actually run. Then write down what surprised you. That log is worth more than the next thirty hours of reading.

## A closing note

Thirty hours gave you literacy and a working set of models: demand creation versus capture, positioning against alternatives including doing nothing, unit economics that constrain what you can spend, the direction each measurement method lies in, and the fact that only a holdout answers the causal question. Those do not expire when the channels change.

Thirty hours did not give you craft. You have not written 200 ads and watched most of them fail. You have not sat through a quarter where the numbers went the wrong way and had to explain it. You have not developed the instinct for when a customer is telling you what they think you want to hear. That takes years, and no version of this course shortcuts it.

What you have is the thing that makes those years pay off faster: you will know what you are looking at. Most people never get that, because they start with tactics and never go back for the foundations. You did it the right way round.

Go run something.
