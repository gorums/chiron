# Path to Expert — Hours 30 to 500

You finished the course. You now have literacy: you can follow any marketing conversation, spot most nonsense, and do the arithmetic that decides whether a channel is affordable. That is genuinely valuable and it is roughly what a good junior marketer knows on their first day.

It is not expertise. This document is the honest version of what the next 470 hours look like.

---

## Why expertise requires reps with real money

Marketing knowledge is cheap and marketing judgment is expensive, and the only known way to convert the first into the second is to make decisions that have consequences.

Here is the specific reason. Every marketing decision is made under uncertainty, with incomplete data, against a competitor who is also moving. What you are building is a calibrated sense of *what usually happens* — how much a headline change typically moves a conversion rate, how long a Meta campaign really needs before its numbers mean anything, how often a promising channel quietly fails, what a lead that will never close looks like at day three. None of that is in a book, because it is distributional knowledge. It comes from having watched forty outcomes, most of them disappointing.

Real money matters because it changes what you notice. When it is a case study you skim the losses. When it is 400 euros of your own you will remember, five years later, exactly which ad set spent it and why you let it. Consequence is what makes attention involuntary, and involuntary attention is what makes learning stick.

The uncomfortable corollary: **you cannot get good at this by reading more.** After this course, more study has sharply diminishing returns and, worse, feels exactly like progress. If in three months you have read four more books and run nothing, you will know more and be able to do less than someone who read nothing and spent 300 euros badly.

Budget for that. A few hundred euros spent learning what does not work is the cheapest professional education available in any field.

## The four fastest paths to reps

Ranked by how quickly they produce real consequence, not by how comfortable they are. You will probably do two of them at once.

### 1. Run marketing for your own small project with a real budget

**What it is:** anything you can actually sell — a service you can deliver, a small product, a niche information product, a local service business, even reselling something. It does not need to be a good business. It needs to be a real one, where a stranger gives you money.

**Budget:** 300-600 euros of media spend over three months. Enough to leave a learning phase and see real variance; small enough that losing it teaches rather than hurts.

**Why it is first:** it is the only path where you control every variable — offer, price, page, channel, creative — so you get to feel the difference between a marketing problem and an offer problem. That distinction is most of senior marketing judgment and it is nearly impossible to learn as an employee, because someone else owns the offer.

**The trap:** picking a project so small or so hypothetical that no decision has stakes. If nobody can buy it today, it is a hobby.

### 2. Free or cheap work for a local business, with a hard scope

**What it is:** find a business you can walk to. A dentist, a gym, a trades firm, a restaurant, a driving school. Offer one specific thing, free or for 200-300 euros, with a written scope and an end date.

**Why it works:** you get a real budget that is not yours, a real client with real expectations, a category you know nothing about (which forces research), and — crucially — a result you can talk about in an interview. You also learn the part nobody teaches: managing a non-marketer's expectations, which is 40% of the job forever.

**Scope it like this, in writing:** "Over four weeks I will audit your Google Ads account and rebuild the campaign structure. You will get a written audit, a rebuilt account, and a one-page monthly report. I will not manage it after 30 November. Success is cost per lead below X." Both of you sign it. Use `campaign-brief.md` and `monthly-report.md` as the deliverables.

**The trap:** unbounded free work. Without an end date it becomes an unpaid job you cannot leave, and the resentment on both sides destroys the reference you were doing it for. Also refuse anything you cannot measure — a business with no tracking and no phone-call attribution will judge you on vibes.

### 3. Take an in-house junior or associate role

**What it is:** marketing associate, growth associate, performance marketing executive, marketing coordinator. In-house, not agency, for a first role if you can choose.

**Why it is the highest-quality reps available:** you get someone else's budget, a colleague who has done it before, exposure to the full loop from spend to revenue, and repetition over months. Agencies teach channel craft faster but insulate you from the offer, the price, the product, and the P&L, which are where the important lessons live.

**Realistic timeline:** with this course, a self-run project, and one local client, you are a credible junior candidate. Expect the search to take 2-4 months and expect the portfolio (below) to matter more than the certifications.

**The trap:** waiting until you feel ready. Nobody feels ready. The bar for a junior role is: can do the arithmetic, can write clearly, has actually touched an ad account, is not going to lie about numbers. You will clear that bar before you believe you have.

### 4. Freelance small, scoped projects

**What it is:** fixed-scope, fixed-price work with a clear deliverable. Not "manage my marketing."

**Projects that sell well at the start, roughly in order of how easy they are to close:**

- Landing page audit with prioritised fixes — 200-400 euros, 4-6 hours. Use `landing-page-audit.md` verbatim.
- Google Ads account audit — 300-500 euros. Wasted spend is easy to find in a neglected account and easy to show.
- Google Ads rebuild — 600-1,200 euros. The classic first real project.
- GA4 and conversion tracking setup, verified end to end — 300-600 euros. Unsexy, in constant demand, and almost every small business has it broken.
- Email flow build (welcome, abandoned cart, win-back) — 500-1,000 euros.

**Why it works:** fixed scope means you can price it before you understand the client's business, which is the only way to survive early freelancing. It also means you finish, which is what generates a testimonial.

**The trap:** retainers too early. A 500-euro/month retainer with an undefined scope is how freelancers end up working 30 hours for 500 euros and quitting the field. Sell projects until you know how long things take you.

## A concrete six-month plan

Assumes 6-8 hours a week. Starting September 2026. Adjust the dates, keep the sequence.

### Month 1 — September 2026: build the thing you will market

Pick the project. Something sellable in four weeks, not four months.

- Fill `business-brief.md`, `icp-and-jtbd.md` (with five real interviews — actual humans, not friends), `positioning-statement.md`.
- Build a one-page site with a real offer and a working payment or booking method.
- Set up GA4, Search Console, Clarity, and Tag Manager. Fire a test conversion and confirm you can see it in all the places it should appear.
- Fill `unit-economics-model.md`. Compute your max affordable CPA before you spend anything.

**Milestone:** a stranger could buy from you today, and you know the most you can afford to pay to acquire them.

### Month 2 — October 2026: spend money badly

Launch one channel. One. Google Ads if you have demand to capture, Meta if you need to create it.

- Fill `campaign-brief.md`, including the success and kill criteria, before launching.
- Spend 150-250 euros. Respect a 10-day do-not-touch window.
- Write a `monthly-report.md` at the end of the month, to yourself, honestly.

**Milestone:** you have spent real money, and you can say in one sentence why it did or did not work. Most likely outcome: it did not work, the traffic was fine, and the page or the offer was the problem. That is the lesson.

### Month 3 — November 2026: fix the funnel, get the first client

- Run `landing-page-audit.md` on your own page. Ship the top three fixes. Re-measure.
- If you have the traffic, run one test with `ab-test-plan.md`. If you do not — the likely case — document that you calculated the sample size and correctly decided not to test. That decision is portfolio material in itself.
- Approach five local businesses with the scoped offer from path 2. Expect four to say nothing.

**Milestone:** one unpaid or cheap client with a signed one-paragraph scope, and a measurable improvement on your own funnel.

### Month 4 — December 2026: deliver for someone else, and publish

- Deliver the client work. Produce a written audit and a monthly report they can act on.
- Publish your first teardown or write-up: your own project's numbers, including what failed. Real numbers with denominators.
- Choose a specialisation (see below). Provisionally. You can change it.

**Milestone:** one delivered client project and one public piece of work with your name on it.

### Month 5 — January 2027: go deep on the specialisation

- 20 focused hours on the chosen specialisation — the platform's own documentation first, then practitioners.
- Take the relevant free certification for the tour of the interface.
- Second client, this one paid. Price it at whatever feels slightly uncomfortable.
- Build the portfolio page (see below): three pieces of work, results stated honestly.

**Milestone:** a portfolio you would send to a stranger, and one paid invoice.

### Month 6 — February 2027: decide the route

- If job: apply to 20 junior roles with the portfolio, not the certifications, as the lead.
- If freelance: raise prices, ask both clients for a referral and a written testimonial, define your one repeatable offer.
- If own business: decide whether the project has legs, using `unit-economics-model.md`. Killing it is a legitimate and informative outcome.
- Write down what you now believe that you did not believe in September. Keep the list.

**Milestone:** roughly 150-200 hours in, one paid client, one live channel you have run for months, and a written record of your own reasoning improving.

**Hours 200 to 500** are the same loop, larger: bigger budgets, longer time horizons, more channels, and the shift from executing campaigns to allocating money between them. There is no shortcut and there is no separate curriculum for it. The loop is the curriculum.

## The specialisation decision

Generalists are useful in small companies and freelancing; specialists get hired faster and paid more. Around month 4 you should pick one and be a competent generalist around it. Here is the honest description of each.

### Paid media (Google Ads, Meta, and the rest)

**Day to day:** monitoring spend and performance, building and restructuring campaigns, briefing and judging creative, writing and reading reports, arguing about attribution, managing budgets across accounts. Fast feedback, visible numbers, and constant platform change.

**Who it suits:** people who like arithmetic, tolerate ambiguity, and are comfortable being accountable for a number every week. It is the most measurable and therefore the most exposed job in marketing.

**Demand in 2026:** high and stable, but the nature of the work has changed. Automation took the manual optimisation — the bid tweaking that used to be the craft — and left creative strategy, offer design, measurement, and feeding the algorithm good data. Junior roles that were "manage the keywords" are shrinking; roles that are "decide what we say and to whom, and prove it worked" are not. Learn the strategy layer or the automation will do your job.

### SEO and AEO/GEO

**Day to day:** keyword and query research, technical audits, content briefs, internal linking, chasing links, and now checking what AI assistants say about your category and why. Long feedback loops — months, not days.

**Who it suits:** patient, systematic people who like puzzles and can defend work whose results arrive two quarters later.

**Demand in 2026:** genuinely unsettled, and be honest with yourself about that. Zero-click searches are around 60% and AI Overviews cut top-result click-through by more than half, so a large part of classic SEO — publish, rank, get traffic — has structurally shrunk. What is growing is the adjacent job: being the source that gets cited, building entity and brand recognition, and the technical work that makes a site legible to machines. If you enter SEO now, enter it as AEO/GEO with strong technical fundamentals. Do not enter it as a content-volume play; that market is being competed to zero.

### Lifecycle and email

**Day to day:** building automated flows, segmenting, writing campaigns, deliverability work (SPF, DKIM, DMARC, list hygiene), and analysing cohorts and retention.

**Who it suits:** people who like systems that compound and are happy to own an unglamorous channel that quietly makes a large share of the revenue.

**Demand in 2026:** steady and underrated. Because email is owned rather than rented, it became more valuable as tracking degraded and platform costs rose. Fewer people specialise in it than in paid media, so the ratio of demand to supply is favourable. Ecommerce and subscription businesses hire for this constantly. It is the most reliable specialisation for a freelancer who wants recurring work with a clear deliverable.

### Content

**Day to day:** research, interviewing internal experts, briefing and editing, publishing, distributing, and measuring things that take months to show up.

**Who it suits:** people who can write clearly and, more importantly, can decide what is worth saying. Writing is now the cheap part.

**Demand in 2026:** bifurcated, sharply. Demand for competent general content collapsed — generation costs went to nearly zero and so did the value of the output. Demand for content with an unfair advantage behind it (proprietary data, real practitioner expertise, genuine access, original research) is strong and rising, because it is now the only kind that gets read, linked, or cited. If you cannot bring an asset that a model cannot generate, this is a difficult market to enter. If you can, it is a good one.

### Product marketing

**Day to day:** positioning, messaging, launches, competitive research, sales enablement, pricing input, and a great deal of internal coordination. Closer to product and sales than to channels.

**Who it suits:** people who like talking to customers, synthesising messy input into a clear position, and influencing without authority. Requires more organisational patience than any other specialisation.

**Demand in 2026:** solid in B2B software, thin elsewhere, and rarely a first job. Most product marketers arrive from an adjacent role or from the industry itself. A realistic target for year two or three rather than month six — but the skills are exactly the Part 1 foundations of this course, so build toward it deliberately if it appeals.

### Growth analytics

**Day to day:** instrumenting tracking, building dashboards, designing experiments and holdouts, measuring incrementality, sometimes MMM, and telling people that their favourite channel is not working.

**Who it suits:** people comfortable with numbers, SQL, and being the person who delivers unwelcome findings. Requires more technical skill than the others — expect to learn SQL properly and probably some Python.

**Demand in 2026:** high and rising, with the least competition, precisely because most marketers avoid the technical part. Signal loss made honest measurement hard, which made people who can do it valuable. It is the specialisation with the clearest path to senior decision-making roles, because the person who can prove what is incremental ends up being the person who allocates the budget.

**How to actually choose:** pick the one where you would happily read the documentation on a Sunday. All six are viable. The wrong choice made in month 4 and corrected in month 10 costs you very little; not choosing at all costs you a year.

## Building a portfolio without clients

You do not need permission to do marketing work. Four formats, in ascending order of effort and value.

**1. Teardowns.** Pick a real company's funnel — ad, landing page, email flow — and analyse it publicly using `landing-page-audit.md`. State what you would change, why, and what you would expect to happen. Two hours each. Do ten and the pattern-recognition alone is worth the time. Be constructive; do not build a reputation on dunking on small businesses.

**2. Audits of real accounts.** Offer free audits to five local businesses. Even if none becomes a client, you have seen inside five real accounts, which is five more than most junior candidates. Anonymise before publishing, always, and ask first.

**3. Spec campaigns.** Take a real brand and build the full artefact: brief, audience, positioning, creative angles, media plan, budget, success criteria. Say explicitly that it is speculative. It demonstrates thinking, which is exactly what the interview is testing.

**4. Publish your own results.** The strongest of the four, by a distance. Your own project, with real numbers: spend, CAC, conversion rate, what you predicted, what happened, what you got wrong. Small numbers are fine. "I spent 340 euros, acquired 6 customers at 57 euros CAC, and here is why my first two weeks were wasted" is more persuasive than any certification, because almost nobody publishes their own failures and everyone recognises that as credibility.

**Package it as:** one page, three to five pieces of work, each with the situation, what you did, what happened, and what you would do differently. Honest numbers. No case study that hides its denominator. Link it from your CV and your LinkedIn, and lead with it in applications.

## How to keep learning without drowning

The failure mode from here is not laziness. It is consumption: subscribing to fifteen newsletters, taking six more courses, and mistaking the feeling of learning for the thing itself.

**A small number of primary sources.** Four maximum, and mostly first-party: the platform documentation for whatever you run, one strategist who argues from evidence, one filter for news in your specialisation. Cancel anything you skim three times in a row. See `reference/resources.md`.

**One experiment a month.** Not one test — one *experiment*, meaning a question you genuinely do not know the answer to, run on something real, with the answer written down before and after. Twelve a year. In three years that is thirty-six things you know from your own evidence rather than from a blog post, and that body of personal evidence is what expertise feels like from the inside.

**A written test log.** One row per experiment: date, what you predicted, what you did, what happened, what you now believe. Keep it forever. It does three jobs: it stops you from re-learning the same lesson, it makes you calibrate your predictions against reality, and it is the single most convincing document you can put in front of a hiring manager or a client. The log in `templates/ab-test-plan.md` is the format. Start it in month 2 and never stop.

**A rule for new tactics.** When something new appears — a platform, a format, an AI tool — do not learn it because it is new. Wait until you have a problem it solves, then learn it in the context of that problem. You will learn it faster and you will skip the majority that disappear within a year.

## How to know when you are actually good

There are only two tests, and neither of them is a title or a certificate.

**One: you can predict outcomes before you run the campaign.**

Before launching, write down what you expect: the CPC, the conversion rate, the cost per lead, the close rate, the number of customers. Seal it. Compare afterwards. Early on you will be wrong by multiples. Over time your ranges tighten. When you are consistently inside a factor of about 1.5 on a channel and market you know, you have real judgment — because being able to predict a system means you have a working model of it, and a working model is exactly what expertise is.

This is also the most valuable thing you can do in an interview or a pitch: state what you expect to happen, in numbers, before anyone asks. Almost nobody does it, and it is instantly recognisable as competence.

**Two: you can say no to work that will not succeed.**

The clearest marker of a good marketer is turning down a project because the offer is wrong, the unit economics do not permit paid acquisition, the expectations are impossible, or the client wants leads for a product nobody wants. Juniors take the work and hope. Seniors run the arithmetic, say "this will not work, and here is what would have to change first," and walk away from money.

It requires knowing enough to be sure, and having enough options to afford it. When you find yourself doing it for the first time — declining a paying client because you can show, on one page, that the maths does not work — you are past the point where this document is useful to you.

---

## The short version

Read less than you think. Spend a few hundred euros of your own money on something real. Write down what you expect before you spend it. Keep a log of what happened. Publish the failures. Pick one specialisation by month four. Get someone else's budget in your hands as fast as possible, because consequence is the only teacher that works.

Thirty hours made you literate. The next four hundred and seventy are reps, and there is no version of this where you skip them.
