# M16 — B2B vs B2C and Sales Alignment

**Time:** 60 minutes (25 read · 25 exercise · 10 recall)
**Part 3 of 6 · Expert Layer**

---

## Why this matters

Everything in Parts 1 and 2 applies to both, but the *shape* of the problem differs enough that applying B2C instincts to a B2B business — or the reverse — reliably wastes a year.

There is also a practical reason. Most well-paid marketing jobs are in B2B, and most beginners arrive with only the consumer intuitions they have from being consumers. Talking fluently about buying committees, pipeline math and an MQL SLA is a large share of what makes a junior candidate sound credible.

## Core concepts

### Buying committees: nobody buys alone

In consumer marketing you persuade one person. In B2B, any purchase of size involves a group — commonly cited research on complex B2B buying puts typical committees in the range of six to eleven people, more for enterprise. Each has a different question:

- **Economic buyer:** what is the return, and what is the risk to me?
- **User / champion:** does this improve my daily work, and can I sell it internally?
- **Technical evaluator:** does it integrate, and is it secure?
- **Procurement:** price, terms, contract risk.
- **Security / legal:** what could go wrong?
- **The blocker:** often whoever owns the process you are replacing.

Two consequences. **One message cannot serve all of them** — you need a message hierarchy (M03) with a per-role layer, plus assets the champion can forward. And **the most common outcome is not losing to a competitor; it is no decision.** Committees default to inaction because inaction is safe. Marketing that only differentiates against competitors, never against doing nothing, loses most of the time.

Consensus is the constraint: the more people who must agree, the more risk-reduction material you need — case studies from similar companies, security documentation, implementation timelines, references.

### Long cycles change what marketing is for

A 3-, 6- or 18-month cycle means months of forgetting sit between first contact and purchase. Marketing's job becomes **being remembered and being findable when the trigger fires**, not converting today.

This is what the **95-5 rule** describes: at any moment only a small share of potential buyers — the commonly cited figure is around 5% — are in-market. It is a heuristic from B2B advertising research, not a constant, and the true share depends on cycle length. The implications hold regardless:

- Most advertising reaches people who cannot buy now. That is not waste; it is the only way to be in memory later.
- Response-rate metrics systematically undervalue that work, because the 95% cannot respond.
- Brand-building in B2B is not a luxury. It is how you get onto the shortlist that forms *before* anyone runs a formal evaluation.
- Top-of-funnel work should be measured on memory-linked indicators: branded search volume, direct traffic, share of the "how did you hear about us" field, inbound requests naming you specifically.

### Lead gen vs demand gen

**Lead generation** optimises for captured contact details: gate an ebook, run ads to the form, count MQLs, hand them to sales.

**Demand generation** optimises for genuine buying intent: publish ungated where the audience already is, build recognition, and let people raise their hand via a high-intent action — demo, pricing, trial.

Why gated lead gen degrades: downloading a PDF is a curiosity signal, not a buying signal. Volume targets push the team toward cheaper, broader offers that attract people even further from purchase. Sales works the list, finds it junk, stops working it, and the two teams begin arguing about lead quality. Meanwhile the gate suppressed the content's reach — the thing that would have built the memory the 95% need.

The mature position is not "never gate." Gate things carrying real buying signal — an ROI calculator, a benchmark tool, a pricing guide, an assessment — and ungate thought leadership whose value is reach. Then be explicit about which programmes are demand *creation* (judged on brand and pipeline over quarters) and which are *capture* (judged on cost per opportunity now).

### MQL, SQL, SAL — and the SLA

Definitions vary between companies; what matters is that yours are written down and agreed.

- **MQL** — meets marketing's bar: right firmographics plus a defined behaviour (score threshold, demo request, pricing page visit).
- **SAL** — Sales Accepted Lead. Sales has looked and agreed it is worth working. This stage exists purely to make rejection visible and measurable.
- **SQL** — sales has spoken to them and confirmed need, authority, budget and timing well enough to work a deal.
- **Opportunity** — a deal with a value and expected close date in the pipeline.

The **SLA** is the contract between the teams, and it runs both ways:

> Marketing delivers 300 MQLs per month meeting the agreed definition. Sales contacts each within 4 business hours, makes at least 5 attempts across 10 business days, and dispositions every lead in the CRM with a reason code. Rejected leads are reviewed jointly, monthly.

The reason codes are the valuable part. Without them, "lead quality is bad" is an opinion; with them you can see it is 40% wrong-company-size and fix the targeting. Speed matters too — responding in minutes rather than days materially changes contact and qualification rates, which is why routing and alerting deserve real attention.

### Pipeline math

The calculation you should be able to do on a whiteboard. Target: **£2,000,000** new ARR. Average contract value **£20,000** → **100 new customers**. Work backwards through observed rates:

| Stage | Rate | Volume needed |
|-------|------|---------------|
| Closed-won | — | 100 |
| Opportunities | 25% win | 400 |
| SQL → Opp | 50% | 800 |
| MQL → SQL | 30% | 2,667 |
| MQLs per year | — | **~2,700** |
| Per month | — | **~225** |

Then add the parts everyone forgets:

- **Sales capacity.** 400 opportunities a year at 60 per rep is ~7 reps. With 4 reps, 2,700 MQLs produce a queue and a lead-quality argument, not £2m.
- **Cycle length.** A 5-month average cycle means leads generated in month 9 cannot close this year. Front-load.
- **Cost.** At a £250 blended cost per MQL, that is £675,000 to generate £2m of first-year ARR. Whether that is acceptable depends on margin, retention and payback (M05, M17).
- **The mix.** Not all 100 come from new MQLs — expansion, partners, referrals and outbound contribute. Marketing should own a number reflecting what it actually sources, defined separately from what it influences.

Run this with your real rates and it will usually show the target is unreachable at current budget or headcount. Saying so early, with the arithmetic, is the most valuable thing a marketer does in annual planning.

### ABM, briefly

**Account-based marketing** inverts the funnel: pick a defined list of target accounts, then market to the committee inside each with tailored content and coordinated sales outreach. Tiers are common — 1:1 for a handful of strategic accounts, 1:few for clusters sharing a problem, 1:many programmatic for a few hundred.

Worth it when deal sizes are large (roughly six figures up), the addressable market is small enough to enumerate, committees are complex, and sales and marketing can genuinely coordinate. Not worth it for low-value, high-volume, self-serve products. The metrics change too: account engagement, meetings with target accounts, in-list pipeline — not MQLs.

### Sales enablement content

Most of the buying journey happens in conversations you are not in, so the content that travels through the deal matters as much as the content that starts it: one-pagers per persona and per objection, written to be forwarded; case studies segmented by industry and size, with numbers; competitive battlecards stating rivals' strengths honestly; ROI templates the champion uses to justify the spend internally; security and compliance packs so the deal does not stall in review; and a "why now" narrative aimed at the do-nothing default.

A quick alignment test: ask three reps what they send after a first call. Wildly different answers, or "whatever I made myself," means the enablement layer does not exist.

### CRM and lifecycle stages

The CRM is the system of record: contacts, accounts, opportunities, activities. A single **lifecycle stage** field moves forward only — Subscriber → Lead → MQL → SAL → SQL → Opportunity → Customer — and is the backbone of every pipeline report you will build.

Non-negotiables: one agreed definition per stage; timestamps on every change, so you can compute velocity and conversion rates; original source captured at creation and never overwritten; reason codes on every rejection and lost deal. Without disciplined stage data the CRM cannot answer "which channel produces customers, not just leads" — the question the whole department is judged on.

### Attribution in long cycles

Everything hard about attribution (M13) is harder here. Cycles outlast cookie lifetimes, consent windows and often the buyer's job, and committee members touch you separately while only one fills the form.

Practical stance: track touchpoints at **contact and account level** in the CRM, not just web sessions — account is the truer unit in B2B. Use multi-touch reporting as directional only, remembering GA4 retains three models (data-driven and two last-click variants), none of which see sales conversations. Lean harder on **self-reported attribution**, which catches podcasts, communities and events no pixel records. Judge demand-creation work on pipeline created over quarters, and run holdouts where you can (M15) — by geography, industry or account tier.

### What transfers to B2C

| | B2B | B2C |
|---|---|---|
| Decision unit | Committee, 6–11 people | One person |
| Cycle | Weeks to years | Seconds to weeks |
| Driver | Risk reduction, consensus, career safety | Emotion, identity, convenience, price |
| Volume | Hundreds of leads | Thousands to millions of transactions |
| Price sensitivity | Lower per unit; procurement negotiates | High; small differences move conversion |
| Testing | Rarely enough volume | Often enough volume (M14) |
| Seasonality | Budget cycles, quarter-end, fiscal year | Holidays, weather, paydays, culture |
| Measurement | Long lag, CRM-based, self-reported | Fast, transactional, platform-inflated |

What transfers both ways: positioning against alternatives including doing nothing; the primacy of the offer; retention economics; and the fact that people buy from brands they already recognise. What does not: cadence, creative register, and the assumption that a fast conversion rate says anything about long-cycle health.

Note the blur. Product-led SaaS sells to an individual like B2C and expands into a contract like B2B; considered consumer purchases — mortgages, cars, universities — have committees (families), long cycles and enablement content. Ask about decision unit and cycle length rather than which label the company uses.

## How it works in practice

A functioning B2B marketing–sales relationship has five artefacts, and you can audit any company by asking for them:

1. **A written ICP** and a target account list derived from it (M02).
2. **Agreed stage definitions and an SLA** with commitments both ways.
3. **A pipeline model** shared with sales and finance, updated with real rates each quarter.
4. **A closed-loop report**: which sources produced opportunities and revenue, not leads.
5. **A recurring joint meeting** reviewing rejected leads, lost-deal reasons, and what buyers actually said.

The fifth is where the compounding happens. Marketers who sit in on sales calls write better copy than marketers who read dashboards. Two calls a week is enough to change everything you produce.

## 2026 reality check

- **Black-box ad platforms punish bad lead definitions.** Performance Max and Advantage+ optimise toward whatever conversion you feed them: feed "form fill" and they find the cheapest form-fillers alive. Feed offline conversion imports back from the CRM — SQLs and closed-won, with values — and the same algorithms work in your favour. In B2B, **the offline conversion feed is the highest-leverage technical task in paid media.**
- **AI search changed how committees research.** With ~60% of Google searches ending click-free and AI Overviews cutting top-result CTR sharply, buyers arrive later and better informed, often having asked an assistant which vendors to consider. Being named in that answer is a distribution channel (M08, M18), which makes third-party mentions, review sites and comparison pages more valuable than another gated ebook.
- **Consent rules unchanged.** GDPR and ePrivacy still govern how you collect and email B2B contacts in the EU/UK; legitimate interest is not blanket permission, and buying lists remains a bad idea legally and practically.

## Common mistakes

- **Optimising for MQL volume** with no visibility into what happens after the handoff.
- **One message for the whole committee,** usually written for the user and useless to the CFO.
- **Ignoring "do nothing"** as the main competitor.
- **Gating everything,** suppressing reach and filling the CRM with curiosity.
- **No SLA,** so lead quality is an argument rather than a measurable.
- **A pipeline model with no sales capacity check,** then surprise that leads sat untouched.
- **Cutting brand spend in B2B** because it does not produce this quarter's MQLs, then wondering why win rates fall.
- **Never listening to a sales call.**

## Exercise (25 minutes)

For **your chosen business** (if it is B2C, do questions 1 and 6 for a plausible B2B version — this is a transferable-skill exercise):

1. **Map the buying committee.** Every role, the one question each asks, and the single asset that answers it. Mark the likeliest blocker.
2. **Write your stage definitions**: MQL, SAL, SQL, Opportunity — one sentence each, with the specific behaviour or firmographic that qualifies.
3. **Draft the SLA** in five lines: marketing's volume and quality commitment, sales' response time, attempts, disposition requirement, joint review cadence.
4. **Do the pipeline math.** From a revenue target, using your real or best-estimated rates, back to monthly MQLs. Add the two reality checks — sales capacity and cycle length — and state whether the target is achievable and what would have to change.
5. **Classify your programmes** into creation and capture, giving each the metric it will be judged on. Flag any gated asset that should be ungated.
6. **The 95-5 test.** Two sentences you would say to a sceptical CEO to justify spending on people who cannot buy this quarter, including the leading indicator you will report instead of MQLs.

## If you remember one thing

**In B2B you are selling to a committee that is mostly not in the market, whose default is to do nothing.** So the work is being remembered until the trigger fires, and giving your champion the material to win an internal argument you will never attend.
