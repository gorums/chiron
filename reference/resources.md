# Resources

Deliberately short. Everything here has earned its place; anything not here was left out on purpose.

**Read this first.** Most marketing content online is low value. Not fraudulent — just optimised for something other than teaching you. The economics are simple: marketing content is cheap to produce, ranks well, and converts to agency leads, course sales, and software trials. So the internet is full of confident, well-designed articles written by people who have never spent their own money on the thing they are describing.

Three filters that will save you hundreds of hours:

1. **Does the author have skin in the game?** Have they spent real budget, and do they report the losses? A piece that names a failure and what it cost is worth ten that do not.
2. **Are there numbers with denominators?** "We increased conversions 300%" from 1 to 4 is not a finding. Look for sample sizes, time periods, and spend levels. Their absence is usually deliberate.
3. **Would this have been written if the author had nothing to sell?** Almost every "ultimate guide to X" exists to rank for X and sell a tool for X. That does not make it wrong, but it explains why it will never tell you that you probably do not need X.

One more, harder rule: **survivorship bias makes case studies nearly useless as instruction.** The identical playbook failed for a hundred companies you will never read about. Read case studies for mechanisms and for what was true about their situation, never as a recipe.

## Books worth the time

Six. Each with who it is for and roughly how long it takes.

**Obviously Awesome** — April Dunford
*Positioning, done as a practical method rather than a philosophy.* The clearest book on the hardest topic in marketing. ~4 hours. **For everyone. Read this first.** If you read only one thing on this page, this is it.

**How Brands Grow** — Byron Sharp
*What the evidence actually says about how brands grow: penetration over loyalty, mental and physical availability, reach over precision targeting.* Dry, repetitive, and corrective — it will contradict most of what you have absorbed from marketing content online, and it has the data. ~8 hours. **For anyone who will ever argue about a budget.**

**Building a StoryBrand** — Donald Miller
*A formula for clear messaging when you are staring at a blank page.* Thin as theory and genuinely useful as a tool; the "customer is the hero, you are the guide" frame fixes about 60% of bad website copy. ~3 hours. **For anyone who has to write a homepage this month.** Use it and then outgrow it.

**Good Strategy Bad Strategy** — Richard Rumelt
*Not a marketing book. The best explanation of why most "strategies" are lists of goals, and what a real one looks like: diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent action.* ~7 hours. **For anyone who wants to be more than a channel operator.** This is the book behind the one-page plan template.

**The Mom Test** — Rob Fitzpatrick
*How to talk to customers so they tell you the truth instead of being nice to you.* Short, blunt, immediately applicable. ~2.5 hours. **For anyone about to run their first customer interviews.** Read it before you do the M02 interviews, not after.

**Alchemy** — Rory Sutherland
*The case for psychology and the non-obvious, as a counterweight to everything else on this list.* Undisciplined and occasionally infuriating; also the best available inoculation against thinking marketing is only arithmetic. ~6 hours. **For after you have the fundamentals**, not before — the ideas are dangerous without a grounding in measurement.

**On not reading more:** the marginal book after these six is worth much less than an hour spent running a real campaign with your own money. Books are for frameworks. Reps are for skill. Do not use reading as a way to avoid the uncomfortable part.

## Free courses and certifications

**Be honest about what these are.** Certifications are weak signals. No hiring manager has ever been impressed by a Google Ads certification, because everyone knows the exams are multiple-choice, repeatable, and gameable. What they are is a free, structured tour of a platform's own vocabulary and interface, which is genuinely useful when you have never opened the tool. Do them for the tour, list them in a footer line on your CV, and do not expect them to open doors.

**Google Skillshop** — free. Certifications for Google Ads (Search, Display, Video, Shopping, Measurement) and Google Analytics. Do **Google Ads Search** and **GA4** if you will touch either. Roughly 4-6 hours each. The GA4 material is the fastest way to stop being confused by events versus key events.

**HubSpot Academy** — free. Inbound marketing, email, content. The content is competent and quite general. The **Email Marketing** course is the most useful one; the inbound certification is mostly HubSpot's worldview, which is a worldview, not a fact.

**Meta Blueprint** — free courses, paid exams. The free courses are worth skimming for how Advantage+ and the ad structures actually work. Do not pay for the exam.

**Google Digital Garage / Fundamentals of Digital Marketing** — free, very broad, very shallow. Skip it if you are doing this course; it covers the same ground with less depth.

**Microsoft Clarity and Search Console documentation** — not courses, but the official docs for both are short, readable, and better than any third-party guide about them.

**Where certifications do help:** getting past an automated CV screen for junior in-house roles, and reassuring a small local business client that you are not making it up. Both are real. Neither is skill.

## Free tools every marketer should have

You can do professional work with only these. Set them up before you buy anything.

| Tool | What it is for | Set up when |
|------|----------------|-------------|
| **Google Search Console** | What queries you appear for, what gets clicked, indexing problems, Core Web Vitals field data | Day one, on every site you touch |
| **Google Analytics 4** | Traffic, key events, funnels. Free and universal, however you feel about it | Day one, and verify a test conversion end to end |
| **Microsoft Clarity** | Session recordings and heatmaps, genuinely free with no volume cap | Before your first landing page audit |
| **Google Trends** | Seasonality, relative interest, whether a category is growing or dying | Before choosing a market or writing a plan |
| **Google Keyword Planner** | Search volumes and CPC estimates. Volumes are bucketed and imprecise; the CPC estimates are the more useful half | During keyword research; needs an Ads account |
| **Ahrefs Webmaster Tools** | Free tier for sites you verify: backlinks, site audit, some keyword data | When you start doing SEO properly |
| **PostHog** (free tier) | Product analytics, funnels, session replay, feature flags. Generous free tier | If there is a product or app, not just a site |
| **Google Tag Manager** | Deploying and debugging tracking without touching code | The first time tracking breaks, which will be soon |
| **PageSpeed Insights** | Core Web Vitals, lab and field | During any landing page audit |
| **A sample size calculator** | Deciding whether you can test at all | Before every A/B test |

Two paid tools worth the money eventually, and not before: a real SEO suite (Ahrefs or Semrush, roughly 100-130 EUR/month) once SEO is a named part of your plan, and an email platform once your list is over a few hundred people. Everything else can wait longer than vendors would like you to believe.

## Newsletters and blogs worth following

Keep this list small. A large reading list produces the feeling of learning and none of the substance.

**Marketing strategy and general**

- **Marketing Week** (Mark Ritson's columns in particular) — evidence-driven, combative, and a reliable antidote to fashion. Frequently paywalled and frequently worth it.
- **The Ehrenberg-Bass Institute** publications — the research behind *How Brands Grow*. Dense; read the summaries.

**Search and AI search**

- **Google Search Central Blog** — the primary source. Read what Google actually says before you read anyone's interpretation of it.
- **Search Engine Land** — reliable news, moderate signal-to-noise. Read the news, skip the guides.
- **Aleyda Solis (Crawling Mondays / SEOFOMO)** — the best single filter on SEO news, including the AEO/GEO shift.

**Paid media**

- **Google Ads and Meta official product announcements** — again, primary source first. Platform changes are the main external force on your results.
- **Practitioner accounts on LinkedIn who post their own account screenshots with spend attached.** Follow three or four. Unfollow anyone whose posts are engagement-bait formatted as one-sentence paragraphs down the page.

**Analytics and measurement**

- **The GA4 and Google Ads help documentation.** Unglamorous, primary, and where most measurement arguments get settled.

**How to actually use this list:** subscribe to four things maximum. Give each one a month. Cancel anything you have skimmed rather than read three times in a row. The correct number of marketing newsletters is the number you actually read.

## Communities

Communities are for questions with no documentation and for finding out what is currently broken in a platform. They are bad for strategy, because the median answer is confident and wrong.

- **r/PPC** — genuinely useful for Google and Meta ads operational problems. Practitioners, real accounts, low tolerance for nonsense.
- **r/SEO and r/bigseo** — mixed. Useful for "is this a bug or is it me" questions; ignore all strategic advice.
- **Google Ads and Search Central help communities** — official-adjacent, occasionally staffed by Google product experts.
- **A local business or trade community in your actual market** — worth more than all of the above combined if you intend to freelance or run your own thing. That is where the clients and the real problems are.
- **One paid community, maybe, later.** Some are excellent. Most are a subscription to a Slack you will mute in three weeks. Do not join one until you have a specific recurring question the free ones cannot answer.

## Skip these

Time sinks that feel productive.

**"Growth hacking" content.** Almost entirely tactics that worked once, on a platform loophole, for a company in a situation unlike yours, and were then written up as principles.

**Marketing content about marketing content.** The largest low-value category on the internet. If the case study is about a marketing agency growing a marketing agency, the audience is people who sell to marketers, and the mechanism will not transfer to your plumbing client.

**Most YouTube ads tutorials.** The good ones are indistinguishable from the bad ones until you have enough knowledge not to need them. The business model is course sales, and the accounts shown are chosen after the fact.

**Anything promising a template that "did 7 figures."** Survivorship bias with a price tag.

**Tool-comparison listicles.** Affiliate content, without exception. Go to the tools' own free tiers and use them for an hour instead.

**Conferences, at your stage.** Expensive, and the talks are usually the speaker's content strategy delivered aloud. Go later, for the people, not the sessions.

**Long-form "state of marketing" reports from software vendors.** They are surveys of the vendor's own customers, framed to justify buying the vendor's product. Occasionally a useful statistic; never a useful conclusion.

**Endless course-taking.** The most seductive time sink of all, because it is indistinguishable from progress from the inside. After this course, the next 30 hours should be spent running something, not studying something. See `path-to-expert.md`.

## The whole filter, in one line

Prefer primary sources, people who spent their own money, and anything with a denominator. Everything else is entertainment, and there is a great deal of it.
