# Mental Models

Twenty ideas worth memorising. They are the compression of the whole course: if you forget the tactics, these will still let you ask the right question in a meeting, and the right question is most of the job.

Each has a name, a short explanation, and a line telling you when to reach for it. Learn the names. Being able to say "I think this is a local maximum problem" changes conversations.

---

## 1. Demand capture vs. demand creation

Some marketing intercepts people who are already looking; some makes people aware of a problem they were not thinking about. Capture converts well, costs a lot per click, and is capped by how many people are searching. Creation converts poorly, buys cheap attention, and is the only way to expand the market. Nearly every "our marketing stopped working" story is a company that only did capture, drained the pond, and had built nothing to refill it.

**Use it when:** results are flat despite rising spend, or when someone proposes a channel and you need to know what job it is doing.

---

## 2. The 95-5 rule

At any moment roughly 5% of a market is actively in the buying process and 95% is not. Advertising aimed at the 5% is a fight over existing demand at high prices; advertising aimed at the 95% is memory-building for a purchase that happens months or years later. Both are necessary, but only the first is measurable this quarter, which is why the second is chronically underfunded.

**Use it when:** someone asks why a brand campaign has no leads attached to it, or when short-term ROAS is being used to justify cutting everything that is not search.

---

## 3. Mental and physical availability

Brands grow by being easy to think of in a buying situation and easy to buy. Mental availability comes from broad, consistent, distinctive presence attached to the situations where people need the category. Physical availability is distribution: listings, shelf space, payment methods, opening hours, whether a person can complete the purchase at 11pm from a phone. Most brands overinvest in persuasion and underinvest in both.

**Use it when:** you are tempted to make the message cleverer. Ask instead whether more people could think of you, or whether more people could buy you.

---

## 4. Positioning is relative to alternatives

Positioning is not a description of you; it is a decision about what you want to be compared to. The same product is expensive next to one alternative and cheap next to another. Choosing the comparison set is the highest-leverage decision in marketing, and if you do not make it deliberately, the customer makes it for you, usually badly. The most common unchosen comparison is "doing nothing," which is the toughest competitor there is.

**Use it when:** you are writing a homepage, a pricing page, or a sales deck, and especially when losing deals to no decision.

---

## 5. The offer beats the ad

A mediocre ad for a strong offer outperforms a brilliant ad for a weak one, consistently. The offer is what someone gets, what they must give, and why now. Marketers spend disproportionate effort on the expression and almost none on the proposition, because changing the offer requires the permission of people outside marketing.

**Use it when:** a campaign underperforms. Check the offer before the creative, and the creative before the targeting.

---

## 6. Distribution beats promotion

Getting the product to where people already are usually beats convincing people to come to you. Historically this meant shelf space and retail; now it also means marketplaces, integrations, app stores, partners, and being present in the surfaces where your buyer already spends attention. Promotion generates demand that bad distribution then fails to absorb.

**Use it when:** demand exists but does not convert, or when planning a launch. Ask "where can this be bought" before "how will people hear about it."

---

## 7. Unit economics are the constraint

The arithmetic of a single customer decides which channels are open to you. If contribution margin is thin, expensive channels are simply unavailable, no matter how good the creative. If payback is long, you need cash, not cleverness. Marketing cannot outrun bad unit economics; it can only lose money faster.

**Use it when:** anyone proposes a channel. First question: what is our max affordable CPA, and does this channel clear it?

---

## 8. Leverage lives in the weakest funnel step

A funnel multiplies its step conversion rates, so a fix at the worst step moves the outcome far more than an equivalent fix at a healthy one. People optimise the step they enjoy or the step they control, which is rarely the weakest. Model each step, change one at a time on paper, and look at what actually moves CAC before deciding where the month goes.

**Use it when:** planning a month of work, and every time someone proposes optimising bids on a page that converts at 1%.

---

## 9. Attribution measures correlation, not cause

Attribution assigns credit by a rule. It does not establish that anything caused anything. Last-click systematically over-credits whoever was standing closest to the purchase — branded search, retargeting — and under-credits everything that created the demand in the first place. All models are wrong; they are wrong in different directions, which is why changing the model changes your budget without changing reality.

**Use it when:** a dashboard is about to decide a budget. Ask what the attribution window is and what the model would say if it were changed.

---

## 10. Incrementality is the only honest question

The question is never "how many conversions did this channel report." It is "how many of these would have happened anyway." The only reliable way to answer it is to withhold the activity from a randomly chosen group and compare. This is uncomfortable because it often reveals that a well-liked channel is buying customers you already had.

**Use it when:** a channel looks too good, particularly retargeting, branded search, and affiliates. And before any large budget increase — a holdout costs a slice of a month and prevents a year of wrong.

---

## 11. The messy middle

Real buying journeys are not a funnel. People loop between exploring options and evaluating them, stall for months, involve other people, revisit, and are influenced in places you cannot see. The funnel is a useful accounting fiction for finding leaks, not a description of behaviour. Planning as if buyers move linearly produces campaigns that only work on the small minority who happen to.

**Use it when:** designing a journey or explaining why a long sales cycle does not show up in weekly reports. Design to be present and consistent over time, not to push people down a sequence.

---

## 12. Regression to the mean

Extreme results tend to be followed by more ordinary ones, for no reason other than that extremes involve luck. A great week is usually partly luck, so the next one looks worse; a terrible month is usually partly luck, so whatever you did afterwards looks effective. This mechanism manufactures false confidence in interventions, especially interventions launched in a crisis.

**Use it when:** judging a change made after an unusual period, or when a "fix" is being credited with a recovery. Compare to a control or to a longer baseline, not to the outlier.

---

## 13. Survivorship bias in case studies

You read about the companies whose strategy worked. The hundred that ran the same playbook and failed were never written up, so the evidence you see is filtered to the winners before you begin reasoning about it. This makes case studies systematically misleading as instruction, and it is why "copy what worked for them" fails so reliably.

**Use it when:** someone cites a company as proof a tactic works. Ask what was true for them that is not true for us, and what the failure rate of this approach is.

---

## 14. Local maxima in optimisation

Incremental testing climbs the hill you are standing on. It cannot tell you there is a higher hill, because every step toward the new one looks like a loss. Programmes that only run small tests converge on the best version of a mediocre idea and then stall. Escaping requires deliberately testing whole concepts, which is riskier, slower, and the only way to find a step change.

**Use it when:** your tests keep producing 2% lifts, or when a conversion rate has plateaued for two quarters. Budget for occasional big swings alongside the small ones.

---

## 15. A strategy is not a list of tactics

A strategy is a diagnosis of the obstacle, a guiding policy for dealing with it, and a coherent set of actions that follow. A list of channels with budgets and dates is a plan of activity, not a strategy — it makes no choice, so it rules nothing out. The test is simple: if it does not tell you what you will refuse to do, it is not a strategy.

**Use it when:** reviewing any marketing plan, including your own. Look for the sentence that eliminates an option.

---

## 16. Content-market fit

Content works when it reaches the people who could buy and says something they cannot get elsewhere. Most content fails one of those two: it reaches a general audience, or it repeats what already ranks. In an era where a competent draft costs nothing to produce, distinctiveness and distribution are the entire game and production volume is worth almost nothing.

**Use it when:** deciding whether to publish. If you cannot say what only you can say, and cannot name five people who would care, do not write it.

---

## 17. Retention multiplies everything

Retention sets LTV, LTV sets what you can afford to pay for a customer, and that sets which channels exist for you. A business that cuts churn meaningfully has quietly raised its acquisition budget across every channel at once, without spending anything. Retention work is unglamorous, rarely owned by marketing, and usually the highest-return work available.

**Use it when:** the plan is to grow by acquiring more. Check the leak first — filling a bucket faster is a poor substitute for a smaller hole.

---

## 18. Creative is the targeting

Platforms took targeting decisions away from marketers and gave them to algorithms. What remains under your control is the offer, the data you feed the system, what you tell it to optimise for, and above all the creative. The ad itself now selects the audience: the people who lean in define who the algorithm goes and finds more of. Creative is not decoration on top of a media plan; it is the media plan.

**Use it when:** working in Performance Max or Advantage+ and feeling you have lost control. Invest the hours in distinct creative angles, not in targeting settings you no longer own.

---

## 19. Owned audiences compound

Paid attention stops the day you stop paying, and rented platforms change the rules without asking. An email list, a customer base, a community, and first-party data are assets that accumulate, cannot be taken away, and cost nothing marginal to reach. Every campaign should ask what durable asset it leaves behind, not only what it converts this month.

**Use it when:** allocating between channels, and when designing any campaign. If it produces nothing but this month's conversions, it is rent, not investment.

---

## 20. Taste is the scarce input

When anyone can produce a thousand competent assets, competence stops being a differentiator and judgment becomes the constraint. Knowing what is worth saying, which of forty generated options is the good one, when to stop optimising and when to change direction — none of that is automated, and all of it comes from having seen many real outcomes. This is why reps with real money matter more than more courses.

**Use it when:** you are tempted to solve a problem by producing more. Ask whether the shortage is output or judgment. It is almost always judgment.

---

## How to actually memorise these

Cover the page and write the twenty names from memory. Then, for each, write the "use it when" line. Do this once a week for three weeks and they will stick permanently.

Then use them out loud. A model you have never applied to a real decision is trivia; a model you have used once to argue against spending money is a skill.
