# M02 — Customers: Segments, ICP, Jobs-to-be-Done

**Time:** 90 minutes (35 read · 45 exercise · 10 recall)
**Part 1 of 6 · Foundations**

---

## Why this matters

Positioning, price, channel and copy are guesses until you know who you are for and what they are trying to get done. This is the cheapest skill to acquire and the one most professionals do worst, because it requires talking to strangers instead of opening a dashboard.

---

## Core concepts

### Segmentation, and why demographics are usually the worst basis

Segmentation is splitting a market into groups that behave differently and should therefore be marketed to differently. The test of a real segment is not "are these people similar?" but **"would I say something different to this group?"** If no, it is a label, not a segment.

| Basis | Example | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Demographic | Women 25–34 | Same age and income, entirely different reasons to buy. |
| Firmographic (B2B) | 50–200 employees, SaaS | Correlates with budget, but is not a reason to buy. |
| Psychographic | "Health-conscious" | Feels insightful, rarely actionable. |
| Behavioural | Bought twice in 90 days | Predictive, and you own the data already. |
| Situation / need | Moving house within 60 days | Strongest — the situation creates the purchase. |

Demographics persist because ad platforms used to sell targeting that way and because they look tidy on a slide. In 2026 the platforms largely ignore your demographic inputs anyway. Segment on situation and behaviour.

### Targeting means excluding

Targeting is choosing which segments you serve — judged on size, ability to pay, reachability, urgency, and your right to win. On a small budget, a narrow segment with an urgent problem and an obvious watering hole beats a large indifferent one. If naming your target does not make you slightly nervous about the business you are refusing, you have not targeted.

### ICP, and why personas fail

The ICP describes the **account or household type** that gets most value and is cheapest to serve — not a person. Keep it observable: trigger, size/budget, what they use today, what must be true for them to succeed, and disqualifiers. The disqualifiers are the most valuable lines in it; they stop a sales team burning a quarter on a deal that would have churned. Build v1 from your best existing customers — fastest to buy, least support, renewed, referred.

Personas — a fictional individual standing in for a segment — fail three ways: **decoration** ("Marketing Mary, 34, likes yoga" changes no decision), **invention** (assumed in a workshop, then quoted as fact for three years), and **proliferation** (nine personas means no focus). One earns its place only if it carries the trigger, the current alternative, the decision criteria, the objections, and the customer's own words. If time is short, write the ICP and skip personas.

### Jobs-to-be-Done: progress, not attributes

People do not buy products, they **hire** something to make progress in a situation. The classic example: a fast-food chain found milkshakes selling at 7am to lone commuters. The job was not "dessert" — it was "make a dull 30-minute drive tolerable one-handed and keep me full until lunch." The competitors were bagels, bananas and doughnuts.

So **your competitive set is defined by the job, not the category** — a gym competes with a running app and with doing nothing — and **every purchase is a switch**, even if the previous option was putting up with it.

Write jobs as: *When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome].*

### Buying triggers

A trigger is the event that moves someone from passive to active: new job, funding round, failed audit, a competitor's price rise, a baby, a house move, one bad month. People carry a problem for years and buy in a narrow window afterwards. Much of what looks like a targeting problem is timing.

### The four forces of progress

Every switch is a tug-of-war between **push** (dissatisfaction with today), **pull** (attraction of the new), **anxiety** (will it work, will I look stupid, how painful is migration) and **habit** (inertia, sunk cost).

Push and pull create demand; anxiety and habit block it. Beginners spend everything on pull — more features, louder claims — when deals are usually lost to the other two. Guarantees, trials, migration help, and proof from someone recognisably similar move conversion more than another benefit claim.

### How to run a customer interview

The one rule, from Rob Fitzpatrick's *The Mom Test*: **ask about specific past behaviour, never hypothetical future interest.** People are unreliable about what they will do and reliable about what they did.

Bad: "Would you use a tool that did X?" "Would you pay $50?"
Good: "Walk me through the last time you dealt with X." "What did you do?" "What did it cost you?" "What else did you look at, and why didn't you pick it?"

Thirty minutes, 5–8 people per segment; the fifth rarely adds much. Never pitch — the moment you pitch, they get polite and the data dies. Chase specifics: "it's expensive" → "compared with what, and what did you actually pay?" Record and transcribe; the phrasing becomes your ad copy.

### Cheap qualitative data

- **Reviews**, yours and competitors' — read the 3-star ones. 1- and 5-star reviews are emotional; 3-star reviews are analytical.
- **Reddit and niche forums** — search "[category] recommendation" and "[competitor] alternative."
- **Support tickets and chat logs** — the most underused asset in most companies.
- **Sales call recordings** — objections in the buyer's own words.
- **Search queries** — your site search box and search-suggest. Typed questions are jobs stated plainly.
- **Churn and lost-deal reasons** — why people left beats why they stayed.

Thirty mined reviews beat a persona workshop.

### B2B buying committees, briefly

The buyer is rarely one person: **user**, **champion** (sells it internally), **economic buyer** (signs), **security gatekeeper** (vetoes), **procurement** (squeezes). Six to ten people is common on larger purchases. The champion's real fear is "do not look foolish for recommending this," so an internal business case they can forward is a marketing deliverable. More in M16.

## How it works in practice

A first pass takes about a week of spare hours: find what your 20 best customers share; read 30 competitor reviews and tag recurring phrases; run 5 interviews; write one page — ICP, three jobs, top trigger, top anxiety, ten quotes. Re-run twice a year, or whenever a campaign fails and nobody can say why.

---

## 2026 reality check

- **AI makes synthesis cheap and fabrication easy.** Clustering 20 transcripts takes minutes now. But "synthetic personas" and AI-generated survey respondents reproduce plausible averages and cannot surface the odd trigger that explains your best customers. Use AI on real data, never instead of it.
- **Platform targeting is dying; audience understanding is not.** You no longer hand-pick an interest list — you supply creative and conversion signals. What you say, and who you exclude, are the remaining levers.
- **Privacy changes shrank behavioural data**, putting more weight on qualitative evidence you gather yourself.

## Common mistakes

- **Hypothetical questions**, then treating politeness as demand. The leading cause of launching something nobody buys.
- **Interviewing only current customers.** They already chose you; the growth is in those who did not.
- **Segmenting by who people are instead of what happened to them.**
- **Averaging.** Two segments averaged together produce a product for neither.
- **Confusing user with buyer** — common in B2B and anything bought for children or teams.

## Exercise (45 minutes)

For **your chosen business**:

1. **Draft the ICP** (15 min): trigger, situation, size/budget, current alternative, success conditions, three disqualifiers.
2. **Write three jobs** in the *When… I want to… so I can…* format. At least one emotional or social.
3. **Get real data** (25 min) — either 5 interviews using only past-behaviour questions (write the five first, none hypothetical), or 30 reviews weighted toward 3-star, tagged into trigger / job / objection / delight / deal-breaker.
4. **Tally the four forces** (5 min): two of each. Circle the biggest blocker — that is your next messaging or offer change.
5. Capture **10 verbatim quotes**. Do not paraphrase. You will use them in M03.

## If you remember one thing

**Ask what they did, not what they would do.** Define your customer by the situation that made them look, and your competitors by the job, not the category.
